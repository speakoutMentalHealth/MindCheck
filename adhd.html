ADHD: Understand your attention and next steps

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) may involve ongoing patterns of inattention, restlessness or impulsivity that affect daily life. People experience these difficulties differently.

Important: This page and the questionnaire linked below do not diagnose or rule out ADHD. A qualified healthcare professional considers your history, functioning in different settings and other possible causes of symptoms.

For adults aged 18 and older

Recommended tool: Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS v1.1), six-question screener, developed by researchers at NYU and Harvard in collaboration with WHO work. It asks about experiences over the past six months.

Use the official patient-facing six-question questionnaire from NYU. This preserves the validated wording and its published scoring instructions rather than relying on an altered online quiz.

Open official adult screener ↗

On the NYU page, choose the English patient-facing six-question form (or another officially available language). The form is hosted by NYU, not SpeakOut.

After the questionnaire: A screening result that suggests possible ADHD is a reason to discuss your concerns with a clinician, not proof that you have ADHD. A result below the screening threshold does not rule it out when difficulties persist.

Understand your official ASRS screening result

Complete NYU's patient-facing six-question ASRS v1.1 first. Count how many answers fall inside the shaded boxes printed on that official form. Do not count all answers marked “Often” or “Very often” automatically: the shaded threshold varies by question.

This count stays in your browser's current page only. We do not save, transmit or track it.

For the validated ASRS v1.1 six-question screener, four or more shaded responses indicate that further clinical assessment is recommended. Fewer than four do not rule ADHD out. This is screening guidance, never a diagnosis or probability estimate.

For children and teenagers

The adult ASRS is not intended for people under 18. A child or teenager who has persistent concentration, activity or impulse-control difficulties should be assessed through an age-appropriate process involving a qualified clinician and information from parents/caregivers and teachers, where appropriate.

Clinicians may use tools such as the Vanderbilt parent and teacher rating scales as part of a wider assessment. They are not standalone diagnoses.

What to do next

  1. Note examples of difficulties with attention, organization, restlessness or impulsivity, and where they occur.
  2. Consider whether lack of sleep, stress, anxiety, mood difficulties or other issues could also contribute.
  3. Speak with a qualified doctor, psychologist, psychiatrist or other ADHD-trained clinician if the difficulties affect study, work, relationships or daily activities.
  4. If possible, bring relevant school reports or observations from someone who knows you well.
